
# Designing Database Structure for Week 5
## Abhinav Koukuntla | MSSE 601

### Context
For the Week 5 of the Golden Bike Shop project, i was responsible for designing the Services and Data Design of our team's application architecture. Golden Bike Shop operators four store locations and needs a system to manage inventory, retail orders, rental reservations, repairs, and service bays. The database needed to support these operations while keeping information cosnsistent across all four stores.  
The main challenge was deciding which business entities and attributes needed to be persistent throughout the database. A database that leaves out necessary information could not support the required functionalit, but adding unncessary tables would increase the complexity of the design and implementation. Our team had already established the requirements and user journeys in Week 3 and 4, so the databse needed to reflect those requirements rather than attempt to cover every future retail business might eventually need. We also had a limited project schedule and needed a design that was realistic to implement.

### Options
The first option we had was to design the database around the documented requirements. This approach would include only entities and attributes required to support the functionality established by our team. The schema would cover stores, customers, products, inventory, bikes, reservations, orders, order lines, repair tickets, service appointments, service bays, and repair part usage. Each entity would have relationships that support the required business operations. The second option create a more comprehensive database schema where we would add entities and attributes for potential future functionality. This includes information such as employee records, payment information, and additional bike information like model names, serial numbers, and hourly rental rates. It could reduce the need to design these elements later, but would also expand the initial project beyond the requirements established in Week 3.  

### Decision
I chose to design a databse directly driven by the requirements of the Golden Bike Shop system. This database includes only the business entities and attributes necessary to support the functionality defined in our project requirements. Rather than adding a table for every potential future feature, I prioritized the information required to operate the four stores and support the system's inventory, rental, order, repair, and service bay needs.  
The resulting design persisted twelve entities: Store, Customer, Product, Inventory Record, Bike, Reservation, Order, Order Line, Repair Ticket, Service Appointment, Service Bay, and Repair Part Usage. Inventory Record connects a product to a specific store so quantities can be tracked independently across locations. Order Line allows an order to contain multiple products, while Repair Part Usage records the products and quantities used during repairs. Reservation connects customers to specific bikes, and Service Appointments connects scheduled work to service bays. These relationships provide the information needed to support inventory management, rental reservations, retail orders, repairs, service scheduling, and store pickup operations across all four locations.  
I also separated persistent business entities from application modules. The business entities represent information that must remain available after a request finishes, whereas modules such as the Inventory and Retail module contain application functionality rather than business records. This makes sure the database only keeps persistent information while leaving processing and enforcement of business rules to the application itself.  

### Reasoning
The primary reason for this decision was to keep the database aligned with the actual requirements our team decided on in Weeks 3 and 4. Every persisted entity has a clear purpose within the system. For example, separating Product from Inventory Record allows the same product to be tracked at multiple stores without duplicating the product information. Similarly, separating Order from Order Line allows one order to contain multiple products and quantities. These relationships support the required workflow and operations while keeping the data organized.  
The database also needed to support important business rules. Reservations had to prevent overlapping bookings for the same bike, bikes undergoing repairs could not be available for rent, and parts used during repairs needed to be reduced from the inventory at the appropriate store. Modeling reservations, repair tickets, inventory records, and repair part usage as distinct entities provided the information necessary to enforce these rules on the application side.  
I considered the tradeoffs between completeness and complexity. During the AI assisted design process, ChatGPT suggested additional tables and attributes, including employee information, payment information, and more detailed bike data. Although the addition of these features could be useful in a more comprehensive reatil system, our requirements did not establish a need for all of them. Because of that, I rejected those additions for the initial design. This kept the database design focused on only what is necessary and made it easier to verify that each entity supported a required business function.

### Consequences
The main benefit of this decision was that the database structure was easier to understand and implement. The entities and relationships directly supported the system's intended operations without introducing unncessary complexity. This gave our team a clearer foundation for connecting the application modules to the shared database.  
However, limiting the database design to the current requirements also introduced limitations. If the Golden Bike Shop later needed an employee management system, payment processing, or more detailed bike information such as telemetry for e-bikes, the database would need to be extended. Some information that could eventually improve reporting or business operations would not be available in the initial design. Addtionally, having the necessary entities does not automatically guarantee data integrity. The software side of the system still needs to enforce business rules and meet performance targets.  
If I revisited this decision, I would validate the proposed schema against each requirement and user journey, then review the relationships and constraints needed to prevent inconsistent data. I would also consider performing performance benchmarks to identify potential bottlenecks. I would also consider adding further entities when a new requirement justifies it rather than expanding the database based on what might be useful in the future. This approach balances a manageable initial design with the ability to evolve as the system's needs change.
